Introduction

Realvolve is a CRM built specifically for real estate agents. It is marketed to individual agents and teams and promises to turn an agent's relationships and transactions into a repeatable, on-schedule process.

Dave Crumby, a former real estate agent and broker, founded the company in 2011 and still leads it as chief executive from Greenwood Village, Colorado. Realvolve merged with the real estate software company Firepoint in 2020 and sold the Firepoint product to Lofty in 2024, leaving Realvolve as its only CRM. In 2026, it moved every customer to a rebuilt version of the software and relaunched its website.

Its appeal is control. Rather than handing an agent a fixed routine, Realvolve lets an agent or a team decide in advance how each client type and each stage of a deal should be handled, then automatically carries out those steps. That approach attracts methodical agents and teams who want everyone to work the same way.

Fit

Realvolve is best suited to an agent with an assistant and a high-volume team with staff to operate it.

An agent with an assistant gets the most out of Realvolve because the work it asks for is work an assistant can own. The assistant can design the follow-up with past clients and the steps of each sale once, then keep it all running from the office while the agent is out with clients. As a result, what would swamp a busy agent working alone becomes a manageable part of the job.

A high-volume team with dedicated office staff can shape Realvolve around its own process, so every buyer, seller, and closing is handled the same way, while each member works their own clients and the team leader sees the whole picture. A team that depends on a steady flow of purchased online leads should know that automatically bringing them in requires a separate outside service, which may lead some teams to look at other CRMs.

Both should go in with their eyes open. Realvolve rewards a methodical, detail-oriented operator and takes real time to learn and configure. Recent customers report reliability and billing problems since the move to rebuilt software, so a trial on the buyer's own process is worth the effort. For a new agent, a busy agent working alone, an office with frequent turnover, or a franchise, that investment is hard to justify.

Features 6.3/10

The product's strength is its workflow engine. You can schedule follow-up emails, texts, tasks, and checklists based on birthdays, home-purchase anniversaries, or any of the roughly 130 transaction dates, and they recalculate when a closing date changes. Contact records are detailed, transaction management is thorough, and Google Contacts, Calendar, and Tasks sync both ways. Team permissions are fine-grained.

Weaker areas lie at the edges of that core. Lead handling relies on Zapier for parsing, web forms, and initiating new leads on a follow-up plan; printed mail lacks labels or envelope printing and has no automated letter drips; MLS data is limited to the agent's own listings; and phones use a web app saved to the home screen. Several lead-capture, calculator, and content features could not be confirmed in the vendor's documentation and score nothing.

Half credit goes to features that Realvolve covers only partially. Household records include a spouse and children with birthdays but no pets, and prospects are grouped by contact type and status within a single database rather than kept separate. Lead sources are tracked and charted, but there is no conversion-rate report; deal costs such as referral fees and closing gifts are recorded, but there is no general expense tracking. Showings and buyer feedback are logged for seller reports, but offers are not. The IDX Broker connection pulls in only the agent's active listings each night, and it requires a separate IDX Broker subscription.

Some of Realvolve’s distinctive features sit outside the table. Ellis, its built-in AI assistant, flags relationships that are going quiet and deals that are losing momentum, drafts follow-up messages for agents to approve, recommends workflows, and extracts dates and parties from contracts. Predictive IQ and relationship scores assess each contact’s stage, engagement, and referral history. They earned half credit as a niche feature because they measure relationship strength rather than buying intent. The dialer can record outbound calls, while workflow webhook actions send record data to other services. Texting, the dialer, and Ellis are available only with the Professional plan; the cheaper Essential plan excludes all three. Premade workflow packages are sold separately, but their prices are no longer published.

Lead intake is the clearest gap for agents who buy leads. Realvolve documents recipes for parsing Realtor.com and Zillow leads, capturing web forms, and starting each new lead in a workflow, but every one of them runs through Zapier, a third party, so they earn no credit. Apart from the agent's own listings, which can come in through IDX Broker, Realvolve does not import MLS data, so it cannot alert buyers to new listings. For phones, the help center describes saving Realvolve to the home screen as a web app, and no Realvolve app appears in either app store.

Positive

Reviewers who like Realvolve like it for its automation. Workflows are the most praised feature by a wide margin, and users credit them with keeping relationships and transactions on schedule; one agent wrote, "I like the automation features as well as how much can be customized to fit my particular business needs" (Google Business Profile, 2024–25). Daily reminders and transaction tracking also draw steady praise, with one user saying he now has "a clear daily direction on who I need to talk to and what tasks I need to accomplish" (Google Business Profile, 2024–25). Training and onboarding staff are often thanked by name, and a transaction coordinator called the rebuilt interface "a major step forward—clean and intuitive" (Google Business Profile, Dec 2025).

Negative

The most serious complaints concern reliability. Longtime customers describe automations that stopped working, glitches that never get fixed, and a product they feel has declined. One team that had used Realvolve for years wrote, "We now spend more time submitting trouble tickets than actually emailing our clients," and added that "Load times between pages frequently exceed 30 seconds" (Google Business Profile, 2024–25). A customer with more than a decade of use said, "it was always glitchy, and it has progressively gone downhill. Virtually unusable at this point" (Google Business Profile, Jan 2026); the chief executive replied in person, but the problems were not resolved.

Billing is another recurring grievance. Nineteen reviews describe billing issues: annual renewals charged after customers tried to cancel or before the date they had been given, refunds refused for unused time, or refunds that took months. Three of the four complaints filed with the BBB in 2024 tell these stories and went unanswered. One former customer said support was always slow to reach, "but it was never as difficult to get ahold of them as when I canceled my account yet they kept charging me" (Google Business Profile, 2024–25). Four reviewers on three sites also say the sales pitch promised more than the product delivered.

Support itself draws mixed opinions. Many reviewers praise individual trainers and quick, friendly responses; others, especially those with billing or bug problems, describe days without a reply and no way to reach anyone by phone. Ease of use is similarly divided: even satisfied users warn of a learning curve, as one put it, "Yes, there is a learning curve with the software" (Google Business Profile, 2024–25).

Terms of Service 8/10

Realvolve's pricing is now in plain view: the redesigned home page lists both monthly and annual plans, along with their setup fees. Either plan can be paid month to month.

The free trial is easy to start. Every free-trial button on the site opens a sign-up page that offers 14 days with "no charge today" and asks for no credit card. However, the plan sign-up pages linked from the pricing section ask for a card and state, "A credit card is required to sign up for our free trial," so a buyer who starts from a plan will be asked for one.

Leaving must be handled carefully. The Terms of Use require at least 14 days' notice before renewal. The full Terms and Conditions, available via the Terms of Service link on the sign-up page, state that a customer charged for another year without giving notice has 48 hours to claim a refund, that monthly charges are never refunded, and that "Annual billing can occur before or after your renewal date based on batched bill runs." Reviews illustrate how this plays out: several customers report being charged after canceling, and a 2024 BBB complaint describes an annual renewal taken two weeks before the date the company had confirmed.

The balance of power favors the company. A customer must give 14 days' written notice to leave and receives no refund for unused months, while the Terms of Use allow Realvolve to suspend service at any time, for any reason or none, and to keep what has been paid; the full Terms and Conditions add that the account's data may then be deleted and that remaining credits are not refunded when an account is closed. Refunds for new customers are limited as well: a refund within 14 days of the first purchase requires completing Realvolve's "course-based learning curriculum" and may take up to 90 days. Disputes fall under United States law, although the documents disagree on which state: the full Terms name North Carolina law and the courts of Fulton County, Georgia, while the Terms of Use name Delaware.

The move to new software has been disruptive. Realvolve ran its old and rebuilt interfaces side by side until it retired the old one on March 15, 2026, after warning customers in its product updates to switch before the cutoff. Reviews from that period describe frequent new problems, and the chief executive's reply to one of them acknowledged that customers were still adjusting.

Support 6/10

Realvolve's help is limited to writing: the Terms of Use state that technical support is for paying customers only and is provided only by email, and the full Terms and Conditions say the same. In practice, the help center adds a ticket form and in-app chat, but no plan offers a phone line for technical support.

That rule conflicts with the trial sign-up page, which promises "full support from our team during your trial." Because the terms limit support to paying customers, this review adopts the stricter reading, and a buyer should not expect help before the first payment.

Self-help is solid. The help center has about 137 articles, many updated in 2026, and about 47 include training videos. Support also helps customers set up email records that keep their messages out of spam folders, as explained in a help article.

Getting started now costs extra. The plan Realvolve sold in 2025 included importing and organizing the customer's contacts (October 2025 pricing page). Still, the current site handles import and setup through a demo and the one-time Smart Launch fee. It also describes hands-on coaching as an optional service "priced separately."

Company 6/10

Realvolve, Inc. is a small, privately held, venture-backed software company that has sold a real estate CRM for more than a decade. However, it is hard to reach and has a poor BBB record.

Dave Crumby founded the company in 2011 and still leads it as chief executive; the About page carries his letter to customers. He co-wrote a 2013 book on building a real estate business, Real: A Path to Passion, Purpose and Profits in Real Estate, published by Realvolve itself. The company is based in Greenwood Village, Colorado, according to its BBB profile and Google Business Profile, and its domain dates from 2010. The website gives no phone number or street address; the only address appears in the Terms, which list a Raleigh, North Carolina address that is also where legal notices are sent to the company's outside lawyer. The company is small: LinkedIn shows seven associated staff, all in the United States, and PitchBook lists 13 employees.

Realvolve merged with Firepoint in 2020, and for four years the company sold two competing CRMs. In August 2024, it sold the Firepoint product to Lofty, leaving Realvolve as its only product. Development is active: the product updates list new features and fixes every month from March through October 2026. The old interface was retired in March 2026, and the website was rebuilt this fall. Former employees on Glassdoor paint a less settled picture of 2023 and early 2024, describing moved pay dates, lost benefits, and frequent outages.

The BBB gives Realvolve an F because the company did not respond to three of the four complaints filed in 2024, and it is not accredited.

Privacy Policy 9.5/10

Realvolve's documents state that an agent's contact lists belong to the agent, are never sold, shared, or marketed to, and will be removed on request, though it makes no promise about artificial intelligence.

Realvolve's commitments appear in three documents: the Privacy Policy, the plain-language Terms of Use, and the full Terms and Conditions, accessible via the Terms of Service link on the sign-up page and accepted by customers when they sign up. All three define contact lists the same way.

The core promise is clear. The Privacy Policy opens by stating "we never sell your personal information," and the contact lists section of each document adds that Realvolve will not sell, contact, market to, or share an agent's lists unless the law requires it, and that only authorized employees can view them.

The documents also frame Realvolve's role as custodian rather than as a user. The Terms of Use put it simply: "While we own the Realvolve app, you own your data." And the Privacy Policy invites customers to request deletion of their information, promising, "We'll also remove it if you ask, unless the law says no."

The documents omit any statement about how client data may be used to train or improve artificial intelligence. That gap matters because Realvolve now builds artificial intelligence into the product, and its product page says it draws on an agent's client history, notes, and past messages. None of the documents address whether that data stays within the agent's account, so agents who care about this should ask Realvolve directly.

  • +1The Terms of Use state that Realvolve owns the application while the agent owns the data, so Realvolve holds that data only as a service provider
  • +1The Privacy Policy commits to removing data on request

7 + 2.5 = 9.5.

Conclusion

Realvolve is a real estate specific CRM built for an unusual buyer: a detail-oriented agent willing to invest heavily in designing how every relationship and every deal should run, then letting the system carry it out. That investment pays off best when dedicated staff set up and manage the system, making Realvolve a strong choice for an agent with an assistant or a high-volume team. An agent working alone will find the learning curve and configuration overwhelming.

Its strengths include deep automation for staying in touch with clients and moving deals to closing, clear published prices with an easy trial and month-to-month billing, and privacy terms that keep an agent's client data the agent's own.

The red flags include technical support available only in writing (no phone support) and extra costs to get started. Recent reviewers have also flagged issues with system reliability stemming from the redesign of the user interface and backend software. Customers report automations that stopped running, problems that remain unfixed, and disputed renewal charges. As a result, the company’s BBB standing is poor. These risks hit buyers who rely on Realvolve's automation the hardest, since they depend on it every day.

At a modest price for an above-average feature set, Realvolve is a good value for an agent with an assistant or a team prepared to configure it carefully. Such a buyer should test the automation against their own process during the trial, get every setup cost in writing, and note the cancellation deadline before committing.

Alternatives

  • RealtyJuggler: A real estate CRM sold as a single low-priced annual plan with phone support, onboarding, training, and data import included. It suits agents who want an easy start and personal help more than Realvolve's build-it-yourself automation, though its interface is dated and it does little with incoming leads.
  • Wise Agent: A real estate CRM at one low, flat price that solo agents, agents with an assistant, and high-volume teams can all run. Unlike Realvolve, it includes round-the-clock phone support, onboarding, and personal coaching with every membership, though its trial requires a credit card and its terms allow no refunds.
  • IXACT Contact: An easy, sphere-focused real estate CRM with done-for-you marketing at a price close to Realvolve's. It requires far less setup than Realvolve for agents who want ready-made follow-up, but its support has been hard to reach, and its parent company has launched a competing CRM.
  • Follow Up Boss: A Zillow-owned real estate CRM built for agents and high-volume teams, with an assistant, at a similar mid-market price. It handles incoming leads far better than Realvolve, but its privacy terms allow Zillow to use, share, and market many of an agent's client records, with no way to opt out.
  • Referral Maker: A focused referral CRM that turns Buffini's Work by Referral method into an easy daily routine, with well-regarded support, month-to-month terms, and a lower price than Realvolve. It does far less beyond working an agent's sphere, and its members are being steered toward a successor product while the current apps go without updates.
  • Brivity: A platform built by a team leader to run a high-volume team, with strong US-based support and a vendor-led setup. It costs several times as much as Realvolve for a single agent, offers no free trial, and its owners also run brokerage, title, and mortgage businesses.
  • Real Geeks: A website-plus-CRM platform for high-volume teams handling fresh internet leads, with excellent support and onboarding. While Realvolve relies on outside services for new leads, Real Geeks provides the website and lead engine itself. It costs about five times as much, requires a six-month minimum, and lets teams share leads from its websites with partners such as lenders.
  • Lofty: A broad all-in-one platform with agent websites and lead tools, designed for high-volume teams working a steady flow of fresh leads. It is far stronger than Realvolve at bringing in new leads, but it costs about $3,600 a year, has a one-sided contract with no trial, and renews automatically for a year.
  • Top Producer: A long-established CRM for experienced agents and those with an assistant who work their sphere, available in both a newer and a classic version. It costs more than twice as much as Realvolve, offers no trial, and locks buyers into a non-refundable one-year term, making it easier to justify for agents who already know it.
  • Propertybase: A brokerage-scale real estate CRM built on Salesforce for offices and franchises rather than individual agents. It requires at least ten licenses, a yearlong term, and paid onboarding, and shows little sign of new development, so it serves a very different buyer than Realvolve.
