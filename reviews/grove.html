Introduction

Grove is a CRM built specifically for real estate agents. It focuses on the business that comes back to an agent on its own: past clients, friends, neighbors, and the referrals they send. Elm Street Technology launched Grove publicly on November 2, 2026, after a beta period with members of its older IXACT Contact CRM. It should not be confused with The Grove, an unrelated CRM for neurodiverse users and small charities that uses a similar name.

Elm Street markets Grove to individual agents who want to stay close to the people who already know them, rather than to teams, offices, or agents chasing new leads. It sells a single plan with everything included and presents Grove as a short daily habit rather than a system to configure: the software is designed to identify who deserves attention and handle much of the routine outreach, leaving the agent more time for real conversations.

Elm Street Technology has built software and marketing services for real estate professionals since 2016, and its brands include IDX Broker, OutboundEngine, IXACT Contact, Aiva and VoicePad. Keith Beeman is the company's chief executive. The company describes Grove as the CRM that brings together what it has learned across those brands, and its appeal is a calm, low-effort way for a busy agent to remember people and keep a referral business growing.

Fit

Grove is an ideal fit for the average agent: an experienced solo agent whose business comes back through past clients and referrals, and who wants to keep that circle warm without giving up evenings to it. Grove is shaped around that agent's day, pointing to the people worth a call or a note and keeping everyone else hearing from the agent with very little effort or setup. What should give this agent pause is maturity rather than design: the product is new and still limited, and Elm Street also sells an older sister CRM to the same agent that does more for less, leaving open which of the two will get the company's attention.

Other buyers are poorly served. A new agent mostly needs help meeting and following up with people they don't yet know, which isn't what Grove is built to do. An agent with an assistant, a team, an office, or a franchise needs several people working in a shared database with control over who sees what, and Grove has no way for a second person to work in an agent's account.

Features 3.1/10

Grove is a deliberately narrow product that handles a handful of relationship tasks well and leaves most of a full real estate CRM unbuilt.

Its strength is the sphere. Contact records hold households, partners, children, and pets, along with birthdays and home anniversaries, and the chain of who referred whom. Grove turns those details into a daily short list of people to reach out to, each with a reason. Email is another strong area: scheduled personal and bulk emails with merge fields, tracking of opens, clicks, bounces, and spam reports, a branded letterhead, and a newsletter Grove writes for the agent every month.

The gaps are wide. There is no text messaging, no calendar or appointment booking, no drip sequences, no lead capture or automatic lead intake, and no integrations beyond Gmail. Nothing documented enables two people to share a database, so every team and supervision feature scores zero. Printed mail is limited to label sheets, and the content library holds email templates rather than letters, flyers, or eCards.

Several features earn half credit because Grove delivers only part of what an agent needs. Duplicate contacts are flagged but cannot yet be merged. Tasks are one-off, with no recurring tasks, and the task list sorts but does not filter. Rather than a set touch cycle for each contact, Grove's artificial intelligence detects when a contact has gone quiet. Deals carry commission amounts, and goals can total them, but there is no income ledger, and goals reflect the agent's own pace rather than a full activity report. Deal playbooks supply ready-made tasks for contingencies, closing dates, and the earnest money deposit, without true transaction management or document storage. Grove prints mailing labels but not envelopes, syncs only with Gmail, logs stage and family changes rather than every edit, and offers occasion email templates in place of eCards. Imports remember how a file's columns were matched, which helps when the same lead source sends files again, but there is no named importer for each source.

The three Niche points stem from features at the heart of the product. Ask Grove is an AI assistant that answers questions about the agent's sphere and drafts emails from the agent's notes; the daily list ranks people by engagement, life events, and time since the last contact; and the one plan allows unlimited contacts.

Grove's own help center lists texting, calendar integration, keep-in-touch automation, and duplicate merging as work in progress, and its integrations page still reads Coming soon. The product may fill out quickly, but this report scores what is documented today.

Price

Grove has one plan with every feature included, priced at $60 per month on monthly billing or $600 per year on annual billing, which works out to $50 per month. Annual billing is the typical price used in this report, so the typical annual price is $600.

The typical first-year cost is also $600. Elm Street charges no setup, onboarding, or training fees. Phone support is included, and there are no add-ons.

This review uses single-user pricing for its calculations and comparisons. Grove does not publish prices for adding an assistant or for a team of 10, and its pricing page and help center do not describe a multi-user plan.

How the price was calculated Grove offers a single plan, so it is the standard plan. The annual plan is paid up front for the year, while the monthly plan is billed month to month.

Positive

No customer has yet reviewed Grove itself, so the only evidence is the six BBB complaints about the other products of its parent company, Elm Street Technology. They say nothing about Grove's software, but they show how the company that will bill and support Grove customers handles problems, and that record is better than the complaint count suggests. Elm Street replied to five of the six, and four of those replies settled the matter: a refund was issued, a website was restored, an unwanted account was closed, and a billing error was written off with an apology. The replies were specific rather than form letters, suggesting a company that reads its complaints.

Negative

The two complaints that were not settled both describe customers who could not reach anyone. In late 2023, a subscriber to the Elevate CRM tried for more than a week to cancel by email and phone, heard nothing back, and was billed again; that complaint was never answered. In early 2024, an IDX Broker customer who did not recognize Elm Street's name on a bank statement said calls and emails went unanswered, and Elm Street's reply disputed the complaint rather than resolving it. Both are isolated and close to two years old, but buyers of a brand-new product should watch whether Grove's support lives up to its promise of a real person on the phone.

Value 5.6/10

Grove's typical plan, Grove on annual billing, costs $600 per year, and its normalized feature score is 3.1, so each feature point costs about $193. The price is modest, but the feature set is thin, so each point of capability costs more than the low sticker price suggests.

Terms of Service 9/10

Grove has simple, published pricing and terms that let a customer leave at the end of any month.

There is one plan at a single published price, and the monthly plan runs month to month with no contract. The pricing page says a customer can cancel in the billing settings at any time, and the Terms of Service state that cancellation takes effect at the end of the current term, so the next bill is never charged.

The free trial requires a credit card. Grove's standard trial lasts 15 days, as described in the help center, but Elm Street has extended it through the rest of 2026: a November 5 blog post and the pricing page offer anyone who signs up now free use through December 31, with the first charge on January 1. The longer trial is a limited promotion, not a permanent policy.

The annual plan carries the main risk. It is paid up front; the terms make all fees non-refundable, and once the year starts, it runs to the end even if the customer cancels. Elm Street, by contrast, may raise prices at renewal with 30 days' notice and may update the agreement for any reason, notifying customers of material changes. Termination for breach is mutual, and Elm Street cannot end an account at will and keep prepaid fees, so the imbalance is limited to the annual customer's lack of a mid-year exit. Agents unsure of Grove should start with monthly billing.

Support 7/10

Grove offers phone and email support from a team that knows real estate, offset by a limited library of tutorial videos.

Phone support is included with the one plan, and the number is listed on Grove's support and contact pages, with hours Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 5 p.m. Eastern. The pricing page promises support from the first login so that trial users can get help too.

The help center contains 42 articles totaling roughly 20,000 words, enough to cover setup and daily use, but only eight include a video. Importing contacts is self-service via a guided file import.

One-on-one onboarding and training appear only in the migration guide for IXACT Contact members moving to Grove, which promises each member a personal session. Grove's own site offers no similar support to a new customer; the closest is a 30-minute sales demo. Agents who want a guided start should ask whether the sessions are available before signing up.

Governance 6.5/10

Grove's governance score is the average of its company score and its privacy policy score.

Company 3/10

Grove is a product of Elm Street Technology, LLC, a Delaware company that has built software and marketing services for real estate professionals since 2016. Elm Street owns IDX Broker, OutboundEngine, IXACT Contact, Aiva and VoicePad. Keith Beeman is chief executive, with Eric Amblard as chief financial officer and Bryan Skene as chief technology officer. All are listed on Elm Street's leadership page.

Grove competes directly with its sister product, IXACT Contact, which Elm Street still sells. Its help center describes Grove as the next generation. That leaves current IXACT customers and Grove buyers wondering which product will receive the company's attention over time. PitchBook records an ownership change in April 2024, without naming the buyer, following an investment by Aquiline in 2020.

Elm Street's public footprint is uneven. Grove's site lists only a private mailbox in Seattle as its address, and neither Grove nor Elm Street has an active Google Business Profile. Elm Street's BBB profile in Frisco, Texas, carries a C- grade and lacks accreditation, and its employees rate it 2.5 out of 5 on Glassdoor. On the positive side, the company is more than ten years old, keeps its website current, and has just brought Grove to market, so the product is clearly under active development.

Privacy Policy 10/10

Grove's contract treats an agent's contacts as the agent's property, used only to run the service and deleted after the account ends.

The Terms of Service designate Elm Street as a processor and service provider for the people an agent stores in Grove, and they state that Elm Street will not sell or share that personal data. The license an agent grants covers hosting and processing contacts solely to provide, secure, and support the service. The Privacy Policy, which focuses mainly on website visitors and account holders, adds that Elm Street does not disclose personal information to outside parties without permission, except where the law requires it.

Leaving is well protected. After an account ends, the agent has 30 days to export all data in a portable format. Elm Street then deletes or anonymizes the data within another 30 days, except for routine backups.

Elm Street may also convert customer data into de-identified, aggregated form and use it to train its models and improve its products. The terms offer no way to opt out of this practice. Data in that form no longer identifies anyone, but agents who object to any use of their data for model training should know their rights.

The Terms state that a Data Processing Addendum governs how Elm Street handles an agent's contacts (section 7.1), and section 4.1 permits uses "as permitted in Section 6 and the DPA," yet no addendum is published or linked anywhere on Grove's site. An agent therefore cannot read the document that governs how Elm Street processes their clients' data; Elm Street should publish it or link to it from the Terms.

Section 7.2 promises that Elm Street will not retain, use, or disclose contact data except to provide the service, then adds "or as permitted by Data Protection Laws." That open-ended exception undermines an otherwise clear commitment and should specify the legal uses it intends.

Conclusion

Grove is a real estate-specific CRM built for the experienced solo agent whose business comes from past clients and referrals. The agent's main reason to hesitate is that the product is still new and unproven.

Its strengths are simplicity and trust. It excels at staying in touch with little effort; its price is published, its monthly plan can be canceled at any time, and its contract protects agents' client data and any CRM in this category. Its weaknesses are limited features and its relative newness. It does far less than a full real estate CRM, and no second person can work in an agent's database.

There is no independent evidence yet of Grove's performance for customers. Several promised capabilities are still listed as upcoming. The parent company's public record is weak, and Elm Street still sells an older sister CRM to the same agents, leaving real estate agents unsure which CRM will receive long-term attention.

Grove is a fair value for a solo agent focused on past clients and referrals who prizes simplicity and is willing to be an early adopter, starting on a monthly plan until the product proves itself. Agents who want more capability for the money, or who expect to add an assistant, will find stronger options today.

Alternatives

  • RealtyJuggler: A real estate CRM sold as a single plan for about $179 per year, rated ideal for new agents, individual agents, agents with an assistant, and real estate offices. It scores far higher than Grove on features, reviews, and value, at under a third of the price. Unlike Grove, it can serve an agent who adds an assistant.
  • WiseAgent: A real estate CRM priced at about $499 per year, rated ideal for new agents, average agents, agents with an assistant, and high-volume teams. It offers a more comprehensive feature set, stronger reviews, and support at less than Grove's cost, and it can scale as an agent adds staff.
  • iXactContact: Elm Street's older CRM, owned by the same parent as Grove, sells for about $561 per year and is suited to average agents and those with an assistant. It scores more than twice as high on features at a lower price, though buyers should weigh which of the two products Elm Street will favor.
  • FollowUpBoss: A real estate CRM priced at about $696 per year, rated ideal for high-volume teams and agents with an assistant rather than for solo agents. It offers a much fuller feature set and stronger support for a modest premium, but its governance score is far weaker than Grove's.
  • ReferralMaker: A real estate CRM priced at about $468 per year, rated ideal for average agents and those with an assistant. It costs less than Grove and scores higher on support and governance, though its feature score is still lower.
  • Realvolve: A real estate CRM priced at about $790 per year, rated ideal for agents with an assistant and for high-volume teams. It doubles Grove's feature score and serves teams Grove cannot, though its reviews score lower.
  • TopProducer: A real estate CRM priced at about $2,148 per year, rated ideal for experienced solo agents, agents with an assistant, and high-volume teams. It offers a much fuller feature set than Grove, but at more than three times the price and with weaker reviews and terms.
  • BoldTrail: A real estate CRM priced at about $5,988 per year for a single seat, rated ideal for franchises and real estate offices. It is built for large brokerages rather than the solo agent Grove serves, with a much fuller feature set but weaker reviews and value.
