Introduction

PropertyBase Salesforce Edition is a real estate CRM delivered as a package that runs within Salesforce. Each customer receives a Salesforce system tailored to property sales, with contacts, listings, and deals sharing a single database and Salesforce's reporting and security. Lone Wolf Technologies sells it to brokerages, multi-office groups, and franchise networks in blocks of at least ten users and does not market it to individual agents.

The product began as a Salesforce real estate application from a Munich start-up and was first listed on Salesforce's app marketplace in 2008. The company behind it was incorporated in 2010. It later moved its base to the United States, joined the Boston Logic group in 2017, and was acquired in August 2021 by Lone Wolf Technologies. Founded in Canada in 1989, Lone Wolf is now headquartered in Dallas and backed by private equity. Matt Fischer has served as Lone Wolf's chief executive since February 2026. The Propertybase name now applies only to this Salesforce product, alongside two additional CRMs Lone Wolf sells under its own brand.

Its pitch is a single shared real estate system for an entire organization, built on Salesforce rather than on the vendor's own software. A broker can give every office and agent the same system, let managers oversee their people, and reshape it the way any Salesforce customer adapts its own, which is also why it is made for organizations with the budget and staff to run it.

Fit

Propertybase Salesforce Edition is intended for real estate offices and franchises.

A broker licenses one system that the whole office can share. Agents keep their clients private, while the broker sees everything. New inquiries are automatically assigned to agents, and listings, offers, and closings are stored in the same records as clients, so the broker can track every deal in the office. The ten-license minimum that rules out individual agents is roughly the size of a small office, so it is no obstacle here.

A franchise or multi-office group gets the most from the platform's scale. Separate offices and territories can run on a single system under their own managers. Listings can be sent to property portals worldwide, and the CRM integrates with the rest of Lone Wolf's brokerage software. Because the system is built on Salesforce, a franchise with its own technical staff can tailor it to individual needs.

A high-volume team is a suitable fit. It provides the lead handling it needs, with new inquiries routed to the right member, follow-up started automatically, and each member's work visible to the team leader. It falls short of ideal because it lacks built-in texting, click-to-dial, or call capture, which a team working fresh leads relies on every day. A team smaller than ten still pays for ten seats.

All of these buyers will notice the same defects. The system cannot be tested before buying, the commitment runs year to year, setup and training cost extra, help comes through tickets rather than a phone line, and customers describe problems that remain unfixed. The larger question is the product's future, since its owner shows far more public development on its two additional CRMs, which seem to compete with PropertyBase.

Features 6.2/10

The product’s strongest capabilities support a brokerage's lead and deal flow. Leads come in from portals, Facebook ads, and web forms; the system checks for duplicates, routes them to agents by source, rotation, or availability, and starts follow-up plans automatically. It stores buyer requirements and matches them with listings, while showings, offers, and closings each have their own records, allowing a deal to be tracked from the first inquiry through the closing checklist. Built on Salesforce, the system also offers strong permissions: managers can see their agents' work, while agents see only their own. Individual users can also be restricted from exporting data, deleting records, or viewing commission fields.

The gaps fall on the agent's side of the desk. There is no built-in texting, no click-to-dial, and no call capture. The content library is thin, with a handful of email templates, one drip sequence, and no newsletters, eCards, or Spanish versions. MLS search and saved-search alerts work only for customers who also buy a Propertybase website.

Several features receive half credit because they are partial or cost extra. Money tracking, which includes payments owed, cash received, and bills paid, comes from a Financial Add-On that the vendor installs on request and ties to higher-level plans. Earnest money fields are included with the Dotloop integration (a separate Lone Wolf product), and an outside-agent portal for new-development sales is an add-on. Word mail merge and sharing a single record with a colleague are documented only for the older Classic interface, which still uses the same data. Email from Gmail or Outlook is logged to contact records, but mail is not synced both ways.

Much of what is missing can be built because the system is Salesforce underneath, but only the features the vendor supplies are ready-made. Lone Wolf publishes instructions for building an alert when a contact has had no activity for a set time, which is how a touch cycle would work, and suggests custom fields for commission calculations; both are projects for the customer's administrator rather than finished features. Leads from property portals are read from their notification emails, and support will add a parser for a new portal, but files from a lead source are mapped by hand each time they are imported.

Reporting depends on how much a brokerage chooses to build. The vendor offers a free report package with monthly pending and closed transactions by side, plus agent and company performance dashboards. However, it provides no goal tracking, ready-made agent activity reports, or agent rankings. A Showings Report email keeps sellers updated on feedback, while comparative market analysis templates display selected comparables but don’t calculate a value.

The product page lists features that the help center doesn’t describe. Lone Wolf says its Authentisign e-signature is built in and supports multi-office structures, so both receive credit here. It also advertises Salesforce Agentforce AI for lead scoring and deal predictions, but there’s no documentation explaining how it works or whether it costs extra. As a result, we credit only the built-in, activity-based lead score. Contacts are limited by data storage, not a fixed count, and developers have access to the full Salesforce API.

The typical first-year cost is $10,680. Implementation, onboarding, and training are sold as separate professional services on the order form, but no price is published, so buyers can’t add them; they should expect these services to increase the first-year cost.

This review uses single-user pricing for most calculations and comparisons, which, in this context, means the ten-license minimum. A single agent, an agent adding an assistant, and a team of ten all pay the same $10,680 per year; each license beyond ten adds $89 per month.

How the price was calculated The Salesforce Edition is the only plan the vendor sells under the Propertybase name, so it is the standard plan. The $89 per user per month price comes from the vendor's product page, and the ten-license minimum is from the AppExchange listing; the annual figure is 10 × $89 × 12. Agent CRM Hub prices a single seat at $1,068 per year, but that excludes the minimum stated on the vendor's own listing. Implementation and onboarding are confirmed as separate paid services by the Professional Services Addendum and FitSmallBusiness, but no amounts are published. Prices are quoted monthly, but each subscription runs for a year, and fees are non-refundable, so leaving early does not reduce the bill.

Positive

Happy customers describe a system that brings a lot together in one place: contacts, listings, email campaigns, and drip sequences, all built on Salesforce and customizable to their brokerage. Ease of use earned more praise than complaints, particularly from agents who used it daily after setup. Several early customers also credited the onboarding and training they received. Support feedback is positive but mixed, and comes almost entirely from 2016 to 2018. Much of it comes from a burst of vendor-invited reviews in December 2017 and a cluster of Capterra reviews in April 2018.

Negative

The most common complaint is that problems go unfixed. Reviewers describe email sending failures that persisted for weeks, automations that did not fire, saved searches that stopped sending, and an upgrade that unassigned tens of thousands of contacts, leaving tickets open for weeks or months. Five reviewers say there was no phone number to call; others describe account managers who kept changing; and several ended up paying outside Salesforce consultants to make the system work. Even reviewers who liked the product mention a steep learning curve and a heavy setup.

The sale itself is the second problem. Reviewers on three sites say features shown in the demonstration did not exist or never worked, and that promised onboarding, data import, or website leads never materialized. Billing complaints stem from the contract: reviewers describe yearlong renewals they did not expect, charges above the agreed price, invoices after they had backed out, a card they could not remove from billing, and one former customer of an acquired Lone Wolf product who was sent to collections after its billing contact was never updated.

None of these complaints have received a reply from the company on any review site, so there is no public indication that any were resolved.

Value 1/10

Propertybase Salesforce Edition's typical plan, Propertybase Salesforce Edition, costs $10,680 per year at the ten-license minimum, and its normalized feature score is 6.2, so each feature point costs about $1,717. The feature set is respectable, but no buyer can pay less than the price of ten seats, which puts the entry price near the top of the market for an individual. An office that fills all ten licenses pays far less per agent, which is the only way the numbers make sense.

Terms of Service 2.5/10

There is no free trial or monthly plan; prospective customers book a demonstration and sign a one-year agreement. The Salesforce Edition Addendum sets each subscription and renewal at one year. It provides that it "shall automatically renew unless terminated by either Party by providing thirty (30) days’ prior written notice." Under the Master Subscription Agreement, fees are paid in advance for the entire term, and "All fees are non-refundable and non-cancellable." A brokerage that misses the notice date is committed to another full year, and one that leaves early still owes the fees for the remainder of the term.

Prices may rise at renewal with 30 days' notice, or at any time if a third-party provider, such as Salesforce, increases its fees. This has already happened: in June 2026, Software Finder and Luxury Presence both listed the product at $79 per user; the vendor's page now lists it at $89.

Reviewers describe billing practices that exceeded what their contracts allowed. Customers on G2 and Capterra report unexpected renewals, charges above their contract price, invoices that continued after they backed out, and an inability to remove their card from billing. These reviews span 2020 to 2023, but together suggest the company has not always honored its own terms.

The obligations run mostly one way. A customer who ends the contract early, for any reason other than an uncured breach by Lone Wolf, continues paying all fees through the end of the term "as liquidated damages and not as a penalty." Lone Wolf can end the contract only for cause, and disputes are governed by Massachusetts law. Lone Wolf may also end the contract immediately if a competitor of Lone Wolf comes to control the customer. The Salesforce Edition Addendum provides that if Salesforce suspends or ends access to the shared Salesforce system, including when the Salesforce agreement it relies on expires, the customer "will remain liable to Company for all fees owed," with no refund.

Support 6.5/10

Self-help is extensive. The help center hosts about 300 articles on the Salesforce Edition, including dozens with embedded training videos, and Lone Wolf runs group webinars for agents and administrators. When a customer needs a person, the help center directs them to grant the support team access to their account and to submit a ticket from inside the CRM.

Hands-on help is limited and typically costs extra. Support provides the credentials and steps to authenticate a brokerage's email domain, helping campaigns reach the inbox. If Propertybase can’t yet read leads from a portal's emails, the customer sends a test lead so the support team can map the fields and build a parser. Implementation, onboarding, one-on-one training, and data migration are offered as separate professional services; the free help center and webinars cover the rest.

There is no phone support. The Propertybase request page offers only a web form, and the help center mentions only tickets. Lone Wolf's general support page lists a phone number for most of its products but does not include Propertybase. Reviewers on G2 and Capterra report they could not find a phone number, and a 2024 review on The Close also reported no phone or live chat support.

Customers' experiences have been mixed at best. Some reviews describe tickets that remained open for weeks or months, responses that simply pointed to articles, and frequent account manager turnover. Nearly as many earlier reviewers, however, praised the quick support. Buyers should get response times in writing before signing.

Governance 8.3/10

Company 7/10

Propertybase is a product line of Lone Wolf Technologies, a real estate software company founded in Canada in 1989 and now headquartered in Dallas, Texas.

Propertybase grew out of a real estate package for Salesforce, first listed in 2008, and was founded as a company in 2010. Lone Wolf, backed by the private equity firm Stone Point Capital, bought it in August 2021. Lone Wolf replaced most of its senior leadership in 2026: Matt Fischer became chief executive in February, succeeding Jimmy Kelly; a new chief customer officer joined in April; and in May the company named a new chief technology officer and promoted a chief innovation officer. Lone Wolf describes the executive team as rebuilt over the past several months. The Propertybase BBB profile carries an A+ grade, though the business is not accredited, and its Google Business Profile in Boulder, Colorado, has a single review.

The bigger concern is competition within the company. Lone Wolf sells three CRMs: Relationships for newer agents, Front Office (formerly Propertybase GO) for growing agents and small teams, and the Salesforce Edition for enterprises. Front Office appears in Lone Wolf's monthly release notes, but the Salesforce Edition hasn’t appeared in any release notes over the past year. The product page says the package is updated every three to six weeks, yet its AppExchange listing still shows a version dated March 2022, and only three help articles were updated in 2025 or 2026. Lone Wolf has refreshed the product page and posted a 2026 demonstration video, so the product is still being sold. However, the public record offers little evidence of ongoing development. Buyers should consider this a serious risk.

Privacy Policy 9.5/10

Propertybase's contract treats a brokerage's client data as the brokerage's confidential property, and Lone Wolf says it handles that data only on the customer's behalf.

The Master Subscription Agreement states that the customer "retains all rights to and ownership of Customer Data." Lone Wolf may use that data only while the subscription is active and solely to provide, support, secure, improve, and develop the service. The agreement also adds that "in no case shall Company sell any Customer Data." Customer Data is also defined as the customer's confidential information, which Lone Wolf must not disclose to anyone outside the staff and advisers who need it to do their jobs.

Lone Wolf's privacy policy describes the same arrangement from the consumer's perspective. Information that agents collect about their clients is processed by Lone Wolf "as a processor on behalf of our customer," and a client who wants to see or delete that information is referred back to the agent or brokerage that holds it. In plain terms, the brokerage controls its clients' data, and Lone Wolf acts as its contractor.

Lone Wolf also sells other CRMs and brokerage software. The Propertybase agreement gives Lone Wolf no right to share a brokerage's client records with those products, and its confidentiality terms prohibit disclosure beyond the staff who need the data to run the service.

The weak point is the limited window. After the contract ends, a customer has only five days to request a copy of its data in writing, and Lone Wolf then has 30 days to provide it. A brokerage leaving Propertybase should export everything before the contract ends rather than rely on that window.

The documents disagree on what happens to client data afterward. Section 4.3 of the Master Subscription Agreement states that once the export window has passed, Lone Wolf "may delete" the data under its then-current deletion policies, which is a permission rather than a promise. Section 6 of the Salesforce Edition Addendum states that customer data "will be deleted by SFDC in accordance with the SFDC documentation," naming Salesforce as the party and pointing to documents outside the agreement, with no timetable. Lone Wolf should state in one place who deletes a former customer's data and by when.

  • +0.5The agreement states that customer data is confidential, will not be disclosed, and will never be sold (three of the four protections)
  • +1Lone Wolf states that it handles client information only as a processor for its customers
  • +1Customers can obtain a copy of their data after the contract ends

7 + 2.5 = 9.5.

Conclusion

Propertybase Salesforce Edition is a real estate-specific CRM built for brokerage offices and franchise groups that want every agent working in one controlled system. A large team that works its leads hard can also make it serve. The ten-license minimum excludes individual agents and small teams, and even the buyers it is built for should weigh the risks before committing.

Its strengths are organizational: it follows a deal from first inquiry to closing, controls who can see what across offices and teams, and keeps a brokerage's client data in the brokerage's hands under its contract. Its weaknesses lie in the software rather than in the software itself: the contract terms are strict, the help that matters most costs extra, and customers describe problems that remained unfixed, and sales promises the product did not keep.

Two red flags stand out. The product shows little public sign of development, while its owner is focusing its efforts on two other CRMs. That owner has also replaced most of its senior leadership this year, making the product's future harder to read. The customer evidence is also old, with no reviews on any site in the past two years, so buyers have no recent outside account of how the product performs today.

At more than $10,000 per year for the smallest purchase, it is poor value for anyone who will not fill all ten seats. For a brokerage or franchise that will, the cost per agent is reasonable, and the platform earns a place on a shortlist, provided a product roadmap, support response times, and a plan for exporting the data are agreed in writing before signing.

Alternatives

  • RealtyJuggler: A low-cost real estate CRM priced at $179 per year, with top marks in reviews, value, and contract terms, the three areas where Propertybase scores lowest. It is built for individual agents, agents with an assistant, and smaller offices rather than franchises, so it serves the buyers that Propertybase's ten-license minimum excludes.
  • Brivity: A real estate CRM priced at $3,588 per year for a solo agent, with a perfect support score and better reviews than Propertybase. It is aimed first at high-volume teams, with offices and franchises as a secondary market, the reverse of Propertybase's priorities.
  • RealGeeks: A platform that pairs agent websites and lead generation with a CRM, priced at $3,990 per year, with much stronger reviews and support policies than Propertybase. It is built for high-volume teams working with fresh leads rather than for franchises, and its privacy terms are far weaker, with a governance score of 4.0 against Propertybase's 8.3.
  • RethinkCRM: Another Salesforce-based CRM priced at $1,548 per year, with better reviews than Propertybase. It is aimed at commercial brokers, their assistants, and commercial offices rather than residential franchises, so it suits brokerages that prefer a Salesforce foundation but work in commercial property or want to start smaller.
  • Lofty: An all-in-one platform with agent websites, lead generation, and a CRM, scoring 8.6 on features at $3,588 per year. It is primarily built for high-volume teams, with offices and franchises a secondary fit. Its reviews and contract terms score even lower than Propertybase's.
  • LoneWolfRelationships: A much simpler contact manager from Lone Wolf, the same company that owns Propertybase, costs $399 per user per year. It has far fewer features but scores better on value and contract terms. Its main appeal is compatibility with Lone Wolf's other brokerage software.
  • BoldTrail: A real estate platform priced at $5,988 per year for a single seat that scores higher than Propertybase on features and support policies. Its reviews and contract terms score lower, so a buyer choosing between the two trades a broader toolset for weaker customer evidence.
  • MarketLeader: A CRM with built-in lead generation priced at $2,268 per year, far cheaper to start with than Propertybase and backed by stronger support policies, though its reviews are weaker. It is built for agents with an assistant and high-volume teams rather than the offices and franchises Propertybase targets.
  • CINC: A lead-generation platform priced at $11,988 per year for its solo plan, close to Propertybase's entry price, with a perfect support score. It is built for high-volume teams rather than franchises, and its privacy and contract terms score near the bottom of the scale.
