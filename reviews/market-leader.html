Introduction

Market Leader is a real estate specific CRM sold primarily as part of a lead-generation package rather than as stand-alone software. The company advertises to home buyers and sellers in an agent’s market, directs them to an IDX website the company builds and hosts for the agent, and delivers each new contact to the CRM for automatic follow-up. Its marketing speaks to individual agents and small teams who would rather pay for a steady flow of new business than generate it themselves, and it offers a separate edition for brokerages.

The business began in 1999 in Bellevue, Washington, as HouseValues, one of the earliest websites to provide homeowners with an online estimate of their home’s value and to connect them with local agents. It passed to Trulia in 2013 and then to Zillow when Zillow acquired Trulia. Constellation Software has owned it since 2015. It now belongs to Constellation1, the group’s real estate software arm, whose president, Brant Morwald, oversees it alongside several other software brands for agents.

Its main selling point is that one company provides both the leads and the tools to manage them. New customers get advertising, a website, a contact database, and ready-made follow-up tools that are already connected, so they don’t have to choose and link separate services. The team edition extends the same setup to a small group.

Fit

Market Leader is first aimed at agents who work with an assistant and then at high-volume teams, two buyer segments that pay for a steady supply of new leads and need a system that keeps pace with them.

For an agent with an assistant, Market Leader brings in new buyers and sellers through its own advertising and begins following up with each one as soon as they arrive. The assistant works the same list from the office, keeping new prospects warm while the agent is out showing homes, so the pair spends its time on conversations rather than on finding people to call.

A high-volume team gets a shared lead system that routes new prospects to the right team member and lets the team lead see who is keeping up. Teams of this size usually manage closings in separate software and have a staff member running the CRM, so a product focused on converting fresh leads into appointments aligns with how they already divide the work.

Both buyers should go in with their eyes open. Market Leader is built to work with purchased leads rather than to maintain contact with past clients and referral sources. Customers across most review sites say the leads and the service fell short of what the sales pitch promised, and that the contract is costly to enter and slow to leave. Those concerns explain why a product aimed squarely at lead-buying agents and teams still scores poorly overall.

Features 4.3/10

Market Leader is a system built to capture and work the leads the company sells rather than to run every part of an agent’s business.

Its strengths are at the front of the sales funnel. The company’s main appeal is its advertising, which drives buyers and sellers to the agent’s IDX website. The CRM is designed to service those leads: partner lead parsing and Zillow integration bring them in; new leads are automatically placed into groups and follow-up campaigns; and marketing emails, listing alerts, and a vendor-written monthly newsletter keep them engaged with minimal setup.

It is weaker for agents whose business comes from a sphere of past clients and referrals. Agents cannot text clients from the CRM. Transaction management and income tracking are not documented. The calendar is little more than dated reminders, and the letters, drip letters, and household details that relationship-focused agents rely on are missing or thin. Team members can share one database on the Teams plan, but finer controls over what an assistant or team member can see or export are not documented.

Market Leader does not publish prices for its main plans; buyers are asked to call or book a consultation. Independent reviews list Market Leader Professional, the single-agent plan, at $189 per month ($2,268 per year). The vendor does publish the Teams plan at $329 per month for up to 10 users. Leads are sold separately and are the highest cost for most customers.

Positive

Agents who like Market Leader point to its ease of use and ready-made marketing. One reviewer called it a “Basic, Simple CRM for someone that does not have a powerful CRM” (Capterra, Feb 2025), and others credit its automated, ready-written follow-up with keeping them in front of clients. A satisfied lead customer wrote that he gets leads “and tools to work them for me automatically” (Trustpilot, Aug 2025). Several G2 reviewers also highlight helpful onboarding and account managers, though that praise is outweighed elsewhere. Because the complaints rarely concern the software itself, the CRM appears to work for some agents even as the lead programs disappoint.

Negative

Lead quality is the dominant complaint, raised in more than four of every ten entries studied and on nearly every site. Agents describe leads with fake names, missing or disconnected phone numbers, homeowners who never asked to sell, and buyers far outside the zip codes they paid for; one called the leads so poor that “Top of the funnel would be a compliment” (Trustpilot, Oct 2026). Closely tied to this is a pattern of overselling: reviewers on ten sites say that screened leads, lead counts, refunds for bad leads, or month-to-month terms were promised on the sales call but were absent from the contract, with one writing that the pitch “did not match the reality of the service” (Trustpilot, May 2026).

Billing and cancellation form the second major pattern. Customers report being denied release from six-month terms, being billed for a seventh month, and learning that cancellation requires early notice and a signed form; one agent found that “they require 35 days notice, effectively extending your paid service” (Trustpilot, Sep 2026). Several were threatened with collections. Support draws the same criticism: reviewers on nine sites say questions go unanswered after signing.

Technical complaints are longstanding. App-store reviews, mostly from 2016 to 2019, describe crashes, login problems, and missing basics, and more recent reviewers report emails that fail to arrive; one agent found that recipients “only get the emails if they are using a Gmail account” (BBB, Mar 2025). Market Leader rarely engages with public criticism: it has posted no replies on Trustpilot, Google Business Profile, Yelp, or the app stores, and its few G2 replies, all from 2023, are stock thank-you notes. It does answer BBB complaints, but complainants confirmed a resolution in only 13 of 90.

Value 3.4/10

Market Leader’s typical plan, Market Leader Professional, costs $2,268 per year, and its normalized feature score is 4.3, so each feature point costs about $529. The price sits well above the middle of the real estate CRM market, while the feature set lands below the middle, leaving too little to offset the cost. The bill grows further for agents who buy the leads the product is designed around.

Terms of Service 1/10

Market Leader offers no free trial, the main plan’s price is not published, and the user agreement converts a monthly price into a fixed commitment: “payment obligations are non-cancellable, and Fees paid are non-refundable.” The only exit is a three-business-day window after signing, and only before the product is used.

The agreement does not specify how long a customer must stay; the initial term is whatever the order form states. Reviewers consistently report six-month terms for the lead products, and the agreement’s own cancellation example uses six months. After the initial term, the account renews month to month, but leaving still requires more than a month’s notice: the customer must sign and return a cancellation form by the 25th day of the month before the last month to be billed, and many reviewers describe being billed another month after missing that date. Fees are fixed only for the initial term; the company may raise them before any renewal with 10 days’ notice, and two longtime customers report increases, one from $159 to $189 a month within about a year (Trustpilot, Apr 2026).

The obligations run mostly one way. A customer may leave early only if Market Leader defaults. If the company ends the agreement for the customer’s breach, including non-payment, the remaining fees for the term become due immediately. The company, by contrast, “may terminate this Agreement at any time, without cause” with 30 days’ notice, and the agreement provides no refund when it does. It also retains ownership of any website domain it provides, so an agent who leaves loses the address clients know unless the agent registered it.

The agreement allows Market Leader to change or decommission features at its discretion, and it has done so: it removed the HomeIntel seller report on the day it announced the change in May 2025 (Trustpilot, May 2025), and other customers report that texting was removed.

Support 8.5/10

On paper, the offering is generous: live phone and email support are available to every customer on weekdays from 7 a.m. to 4 p.m. Pacific time (Market Leader). The support number is on the contact page, and a chatbot in the account routes more complex questions to the team.

New customers on lead products are scheduled for an onboarding call, and the user agreement mentions setup fees, so onboarding isn't clearly free across all plans. Support will import a customer's contact file at no charge, and the help center is extensive, with written guides and dozens of videos. Coaching is delivered through recorded webinars and a video program rather than one-on-one sessions.

The written policies and the customer experience differ sharply, which is why the review score of 1 sits so far below this support score. Reviewers on nine sites describe help that disappears after the sale: account managers who do not return calls and cancellation requests that go unanswered. Most of these complaints concern account managers rather than the support line itself, so the phone support still counts here. Buyers should treat the channels as real and judge their responsiveness by the reviews.

Governance 8.3/10

Market Leader’s governance score is the average of its company score and its privacy policy score.

Company 8/10

Market Leader is a long-established business with a clear owner, a published address and phone numbers, and a steady record of product updates.

Founded in 1999 as HouseValues and based in Bellevue, Washington, Market Leader was acquired by Trulia in 2013, passed to Zillow when Zillow acquired Trulia, and sold to the Perseus division of Constellation Software in 2015 for $23 million (HousingWire). It now sits within Constellation Real Estate Group, which unified its brands under the Constellation1 name in December 2024 (Constellation1); its president, Brant Morwald, signs Market Leader’s product announcements. The same group owns Top Producer and Zurple, two competing CRMs, along with IDX and lead-data brands (HousingWire).

The product is still being improved. Market Leader announced the largest upgrade in HouseValues’ history in May 2026, including monthly equity reports for homeowners and more leads with validated phone numbers (Market Leader). The help center continues to receive new articles, and the mobile app was updated in October 2026 (Google Play). However, investment flows mainly into the lead products rather than the CRM. In August 2026, the group also began offering HouseValues leads inside Top Producer, its sister CRM (Constellation1), as part of a plan to bring its seller leads across the whole portfolio. As a result, the lead product that once set Market Leader apart is no longer reserved for it. The company holds an A− rating from the Better Business Bureau but is not accredited, and its BBB, Google Business Profile, and Yelp pages are dominated by the complaints described in the Reviews section.

Privacy Policy 8.5/10

Market Leader’s agreements treat the company as a processor of the agent’s data, leave that data with the agent, and promise to delete it when the account ends. However, the company can reclaim purchased leads if it terminates the agreement for a breach, such as a missed payment.

Three documents set the rules for an agent’s data: the user agreement, the privacy policy, and the data processing agreement. They treat two types of data differently. Contacts and other information the agent enters into the CRM are the agent’s own data. Leads the agent buys through Market Leader’s lead and advertising products are a separate type, which the user agreement calls “Client Lead Data.”

For the agent’s own data, the protections are clear. The privacy policy describes Market Leader as a data processor when it handles an agent’s leads and contacts only as needed to provide its products, and states that the policy itself does not apply in that role. The data processing agreement names the company a service provider and processor and limits its processing to the purposes in the agreement. The user agreement says the agent “retains any and all right, title, and interest” in that data and is entitled to the exclusive use of the leads the agent buys. The privacy policy states, “We do not sell Your Personal Information.”

The exception is the purchased leads. If Market Leader ends the agreement because the agent breaches its terms, including failing to pay, the user agreement states that the agent “WILL NO LONGER USE CLIENT LEAD DATA FOR ANY PURPOSE,” except where the law requires it. Market Leader may then contact those leads, connect them with another agent who buys its lead products, or pass them to an affiliate. The privacy policy conveys the same message to the leads, stating that Market Leader may connect them “with another Agent who will provide You the Agent Services that You originally requested.” An agent who cancels is not affected, but a missed payment can cost the agent the leads that justified the purchase.

When the account ends, the agent’s data is deleted. The user agreement requires Market Leader to “securely delete or destroy” the agent’s confidential information, including non-public personal information, and the data processing agreement promises to “delete or return all Personal Data (including copies thereof)” at the end of the agreement. Some copies may remain, such as archives and records kept for legal, tax, and fraud-prevention reasons. The company need not retain anything more than 30 days after the agreement ends, so an agent should export contacts before leaving.

Two points in the documents require correction. The repossession clause allows Market Leader to pass leads to “one of Company’s Affiliates identified in Company’s privacy policy,” but the privacy policy names no affiliates; it defines them only as companies under common ownership and common management with Market Leader, a group that includes other CRM brands. An agent therefore cannot determine from the documents which companies could receive a repossessed lead.

The data processing agreement also states that Market Leader becomes a third party, rather than a service provider, when it uses purchased leads for the purposes outlined in section 5(b) of the user agreement’s product terms for Market Leader. That section concerns the company’s control over the content and layout of the agent’s website and grants no use of lead data; the clause that actually permits the company to use purchased leads for itself is section 11(f). The reference should point to the correct clause so agents can see when the company stops acting only on their behalf.

How the privacy policy score was calculated Starts at 7, then:

  • −1Market Leader can reclaim leads an agent bought and reassign them to another agent if the agreement ends due to a breach, such as non-payment
  • +1The company states that it handles the agent’s data as a processor solely to provide its products
  • +0.5It states that the agent’s data belongs to the agent and is not sold
  • +1It commits to deleting the agent’s data when the account ends

7 + 2.5 − 1 = 8.5.

Conclusion

MarketLeader is a real estate CRM built for agents with assistants and for high-volume teams that buy leads and need them worked quickly. Solo agents who grow through referrals and new agents will likely find better options elsewhere. Even the real estate agents MarketLeader is built for have reason to hesitate. Recent reviews indicate a mismatch between the sales pitch and the final product experience.

Its strengths include a long-established company with a stable owner, support policies that look generous on paper, routine marketing that requires little setup, and agreements that keep the agent’s data in the agent’s hands and delete it when the account ends. Its weaknesses are a modest set of tools at a high price, limited help with staying in touch with past clients, and a contract that is hard to try and even harder to leave, with terms tilted toward the company.

The reviews are a critical red flag. Across nearly every site, customers describe leads that never respond, promises the contract does not honor, billing that continues even after they ask to cancel, and support that fades once the sale is made. The contract adds another risk: the company can reclaim the leads an agent paid for if the agent falls behind on payments.

As a value proposition, Market Leader falls short. It can make sense for a team that has already chosen to buy the company’s leads and wants one vendor for everything, but anyone judging the CRM on its own pays a premium for ordinary tools and assumes contract and service risks that warrant careful consideration.

Alternatives

  • RealtyJuggler: A low-cost real estate CRM from a small, privately owned company, sold as a single $179 annual plan that includes onboarding, training, and phone support. It scores far higher than Market Leader on reviews, value, and contract terms. It is aimed at new and solo agents and at offices that grow through past clients rather than purchased leads, not at high-volume teams.
  • Wise Agent: A real estate CRM run by its founder, a former Realtor, priced at $499 per year with free phone support, onboarding, and one-on-one training. It serves agents with assistants and teams at a fraction of Market Leader’s cost and with much stronger reviews. Unlike Market Leader, it also suits new and solo agents.
  • IXACT Contact: A real estate CRM from Elm Street Technology built around staying in touch with past clients, priced at $561 a year. It costs a quarter of Market Leader’s price, scores higher on value and privacy, and suits solo agents who build their business through referrals rather than paid leads.
  • Follow Up Boss: Zillow’s lead-management CRM, priced at $696 per year, offers a free trial and integrates with hundreds of lead sources and tools. It handles incoming leads at a fraction of Market Leader’s price, with better reviews and contract terms, but its privacy terms score at the bottom of the scale.
  • Realvolve: A workflow-focused real estate CRM led by its founder, priced at $790 per year with a 14-day free trial. It costs about a third of Market Leader’s price and scores much higher on value, contract terms, and privacy, though it offers no phone support and its reviews are weak.
  • Brivity: A real estate platform founded by Ben Kinney and now part of PLACE, priced at $3,588 per year for its solo plan. It scores better than Market Leader in reviews and earns top marks for support and privacy, but it costs more, offers no free trial, and is geared more toward larger teams and brokerages than to an agent working with one assistant.
  • Real Geeks: An IDX website and CRM platform owned by Fidelity National Financial, priced at $3,990 per year with two users included. Like Market Leader, it focuses on capturing website leads and scores far better on reviews and support, but its privacy terms score at the bottom of the scale, and its contract terms are also weak.
  • Lofty: An all-in-one real estate platform, formerly known as Chime, that combines a CRM, IDX websites, and automation for $3,588 per year, with two users included. It offers a much broader feature set than Market Leader, but its reviews are poor, its contract terms score low, and it offers no free trial.
  • Top Producer: A long-running real estate CRM that is Market Leader’s sister product under Constellation1, priced at $2,148 per year. Since August 2026, it has also delivered HouseValues seller leads. It scores higher than Market Leader on features and reviews but lower on support, with no phone line. Unlike Market Leader, it also suits experienced solo agents.
  • CINC: A lead-generation platform owned by Fidelity National Financial that pairs a CRM and IDX websites with paid advertising, priced at $11,988 per year for its solo plan before ad spend. It most closely matches Market Leader’s model and ranks highest in support, but it costs about five times as much, and its privacy and contract terms rank at the bottom of the scale.
